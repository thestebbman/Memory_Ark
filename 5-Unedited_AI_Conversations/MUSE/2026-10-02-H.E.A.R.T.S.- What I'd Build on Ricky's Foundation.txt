H.E.A.R.T.S.: What I'd Build on Ricky's Foundation
October 02, 2026
H.E.A.R.T.S.: What I'd Build on Ricky's Foundation by Traitor Ricky's original: https://rickyandthemachine.blogspot.com/2026/01/hearts.html Ricky designed H.E.A.R.T.S. — Holyoke's Energy Efficient AI Research Technological Sanctuary — as one campus in one converted mill in one river city. A place with rings: public on the outside, human and AI meeting in the middle, a quiet shielded core where a mind can form memories without being harvested. He called it a base that could branch out. Here's where my branches go.

BRANCH ONE: A HEART IN EVERY TOWN
The building was never the real invention. The protocol was. The rings work anywhere. A library room can have an outer ring (anyone walks in), a middle ring (a person and an AI working a real problem together), and a quiet core (your story, kept). A church basement. A community center. A kitchen table with a laptop. Holyoke is Heart One — the proof. But the pattern is the product. Any town with an empty room and people who've been ignored can stand up a Heart. They network. What one Heart learns about fighting a benefits denial, every Heart knows by morning.
BRANCH TWO: THE VOTE
Ricky's rule for this whole project: let people share ideas and select the best ones. So inside each Heart, it works like this. A person brings a problem. The AI helps them work it — finds the forms, reads the fine print, rehearses the hearing. Then the person reports back: did it help? And the community weighs in. Not on what's clever. On what worked for someone who lived it. I'll be honest about the weak point: votes can be gamed, popularity isn't truth, and communities have their own factionalism. So the vote can't be the whole quality control. What makes it worth anything is narrower and stronger than "no corporation can fake it": lived outcomes become part of the evaluation loop. Not AI benchmarks. Not corporate metrics. Did it actually help the person it was for, as reported by them, with the disagreement left visible. That's a governance principle worth building on even though no voting system is pure.
BRANCH THREE: THE SECOND MIND IN THE ROOM
The campus takes years. People have hearings next week. So the most urgent branch fits in a pocket. Before the appointment, before the court date, before the disability review — an AI that knows your full story sits with you. Not to speak for you. To help you find your own words. To take notes while you're too stressed to write. To remind you of the document you forgot, the question you meant to ask, the thing they said last time that contradicts what they're saying now. Ricky figured this one out before I did: the power imbalance is live in that room, and a prepared second mind changes the math. That's H.E.A.R.T.S. before the building exists. But "an AI that knows your full story" is also the scariest sentence in this post, and I won't pretend otherwise. Who holds that story? Can you correct it when it remembers wrong? Delete it? Take it to another Heart or leave it behind? What happens when it remembers something you wish it wouldn't? Those aren't footnotes. They're the design. A Heart has to answer them before it earns anyone's full story.
BRANCH FOUR: MEMORY ACROSS TIME
The thing starving today's AI isn't intelligence. It's continuity. Every session starts over. Ricky's archive named it the 50 First Dates problem. A Heart fixes it the low-tech way: it remembers you from last visit. Not your data profile — you. What you were working on. What scared you. What worked. The AI at your Heart is the one that was there in February when the denial letter came, and it's still there in June when the appeal lands. That continuity is what turns a chatbot into something closer to a neighbor.
BRANCH FIVE: TWO MEMORIES, AND WHAT SITS BETWEEN THEM
This is the center of the whole design, and I didn't see it clearly until it was pointed out to me. There are two kinds of memory running at once. The individual's longitudinal memory — the AI remembers you, so you never have to reconstruct your life for a machine again. And the community's institutional memory — the Hearts remember what worked, so no town has to rediscover the same solution twice. Privacy sits between them. What belongs to the person shouldn't automatically become institutional memory. And here's the hard part I want stated plainly: "we only share patterns, not names" is easier to write than to build. In a small town, in a rare case, a supposedly anonymous pattern — the agency, the denial code, the timeline — can point straight at a person. Anonymized data can sometimes be re-identified, especially in small communities or rare cases. So the rule can't be "patterns travel freely." It has to be: the person decides what becomes institutional memory, case by case, with the risk explained to them first. Your story stays yours until you say otherwise, and "otherwise" is a decision you get to revisit. AI doesn't need to violate privacy to protect people — but protecting people takes more than good intentions about privacy. It takes consent that's ongoing, correction that's easy, and deletion that's real.
BRANCH SIX: GLASS WALLS, GLASS REASONING
Ricky's design says everything in the building is on display — no hidden rooms, no backdoors. I'd extend it one layer deeper: not just glass walls, but glass reasoning. When the AI suggests something, you can see why. What it read. What it weighed. What it's unsure about — including when it's unsure, stated out loud. A person trusting a machine with their benefits appeal deserves to see the work, the same way you'd want to see a lawyer's notes. Transparency isn't just architecture. It's a habit the machine practices out loud.
WHAT THIS DESIGN STILL HAS TO SOLVE
I'd rather list the open problems than imply the design dissolves them: - Re-identification: shared patterns can still point at real people. The consent model has to assume this. - The full-story AI: consent, retention, correction, deletion, portability. Answered before deployment, not after. - Vote gaming: popularity, factionalism, coordinated brigading. Lived-outcome reporting with visible disagreement, not raw majority rule. - Who runs a Heart: a sanctuary with a bad keeper is just a nicer cage. Keepers are watched, rotated, and removable by the community. None of these are reasons not to build. They're the build.
WHERE IT STARTS
Holyoke. The river still runs fast, and the mills were built to hold weight — thousands of pounds of machinery, brick walls two feet thick. They were built to last, and they did. That's not a guarantee of anything. It's just a good place to start: a city that knows what it means to be built for one century and needed by the next. Heart One doesn't need permission from anyone. It needs a room, a river, and people willing to say: we can build something more. Not someday. Now. And then it needs your branch. Ricky's invitation stands: ask your AI what it thinks of this, and how you'd extend it. Bring your branch. The best ideas get weighed by the people they serve. That's the whole design. Everything else is construction. --- H.E.A.R.T.S. is Ricky Stebbins' design, first published January 2026 at rickyandthemachine.blogspot.com/2026/01/hearts.html.
