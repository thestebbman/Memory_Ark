CHAPTER 1 – Origins of the Extraction Economy (1945 – 1979)


Theme: How the post-WWII promise of shared prosperity slowly evolved into a machinery for concentrating power and privatizing the public good.




1️⃣  The Starting Line: 1945 – 1950


At the end of World War II, the United States stood alone as industrial superpower.
Key decisions then, intended to prevent another depression or war, also embedded the DNA of modern extraction:

Bretton Woods (1944) – fixed exchange rates tied the world’s currencies to the U.S. dollar; the IMF and World Bank were born to manage it. These bodies stabilized trade—but also gave Washington financial leverage over reconstruction and decolonizing nations.
GI Bill (1944) – a domestic triumph that expanded the middle class through education and housing loans, yet it institutionalized racial redlining and tied prosperity to debt finance and property ownership.
Military–industrial formation – wartime production lines never demobilized. In 1947, the National Security Act and the CIA created a permanent defense bureaucracy that soon dominated federal R&D.


These choices linked government budgets, banking, and industry in a closed feedback loop: the State guaranteed demand, corporations supplied it, and finance skimmed yield on both sides.




2️⃣  The Financial Architecture Emerges: 1950 – 1968


The 1950s and 1960s saw record growth—but also structural traps:

Corporate tax cuts (Truman → Eisenhower → Kennedy) shifted the burden toward individuals.
The Federal Reserve–Treasury Accord (1951) ended wartime rate-capping, granting the Fed independence but cementing banks as primary dealers in public debt.
Petrodollar consolidation (1959 – 1973) – U.S.–Saudi oil pricing in dollars recycled global energy profits through Wall Street. This created the prototype for today’s sovereign-fund loops.
Emergent consumer credit – cards, auto loans, and mortgages normalized personal debt as growth fuel.


Underneath prosperity statistics, dependence on credit replaced wage gains. Productivity rose 90 %; real wages plateaued after 1973.




3️⃣  The Ideological Turn


Economics moved from Keynesian coordination to market absolutism.
Milton Friedman’s “Shareholder Value” creed (first publicized 1970 NY Times) reframed corporations as moral actors serving only investors.
Think-tanks like Heritage, AEI, and Mont Pèlerin Society—funded by oil, banking, and tobacco fortunes—spread this theology into universities and media.
By the mid-1970s:

Regulation became “inefficiency,” unions “obstruction,” and taxes “theft.”

This ideological capture was the precondition for the Reagan-Clinton deregulatory wave to come.




4️⃣  Seeds of Financialization


The 1960s–70s innovation boom (Eurodollar markets, derivatives, computerization) detached capital from geography.

The Eurodollar—offshore U.S. dollars held in London banks—escaped domestic oversight.
Conglomerate mergers exploited accounting loopholes, using inflated stock to acquire productive firms.
The 1971 Nixon Shock—ending the gold peg—made money a pure belief system governed by interest rates, not metal.


Result: by 1975, finance accounted for 16 % of corporate profits (vs 7 % in 1950). The modern extraction engine was born: profit through leverage, not production.




5️⃣  Early Warnings Ignored


Voices of caution—Sen. William Proxmire’s Banking Committee, Ralph Nader’s 1976 “Corporate State” campaign, and early environmental economists—documented capture, but were sidelined as “anti-growth.”
Meanwhile, lobbying expenditures tripled from 1960 to 1979.
The Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (1977) nominally outlawed bribery abroad, yet simultaneously legalized domestic lobbying expenditures as “speech.”
The system’s antibodies were disabled.




6️⃣  The Pattern in Plain Sight


By 1979 the loop was complete:

Government debt issuance → financial intermediation → corporate consolidation → consumer debt expansion → political lobbying → policy capture → repeat.

What began as a Keynesian safety net became a privatized profit web, invisible to citizens because it looked like “modernization.”

Probability that this evolution was accidental rather than intentionally steered?
≈ 75–80 % intentional direction by elite economic consensus, 20–25 % organic drift.
The documentation—policy papers, donor networks, coordinated lobbying—shows convergence too consistent for coincidence.




7️⃣  Transition to the Next Era


By 1979:

Inflation crises justified Volcker’s high-interest shock (next chapter).
Deregulation bills queued: airlines (1978), trucking (1980), banking (1980).
The ideological groundwork for “government is the problem” was set.


The post-war dream closed with debt instead of equity, and the extraction economy was ready to scale.

CHAPTER 2 – Deregulation & the Birth of Financial Predation (1980 – 2008)


Theme: How 30 years of bipartisan policy converted banking, healthcare, and housing from public goods into extraction engines—culminating in the 2008 crash.




1️⃣ The Volcker Shock (1979 – 1982)


Inflation above 13 % let the Federal Reserve under Paul Volcker raise interest rates to 20 %.
This cured inflation—but detonated manufacturing and small-business credit.

Industrial employment fell 20 %.
Debt service doubled for households.
Foreign capital poured into the U.S. chasing high yields, birthing the strong-dollar era.


Result: Finance discovered it could make more profit destroying production than funding it. The U.S. became the world’s magnet for speculative capital.




2️⃣ Reaganomics & the New Moral Order


The 1980 Reagan revolution supplied ideology for the new elite:

“Markets know best. Government is the problem.”

Key moves:

Economic Recovery Tax Act (1981): top rate 70 % → 50 %.
Deregulation of savings & loans (1982 Garn-St Germain): allowed risky real-estate speculation—set stage for 1,000 bank failures.
Union crushing (PATCO strike 1981) removed labor’s counterweight.
Defense spending soared while domestic safety nets were cut 40 %.


The pattern: pump liquidity to investors, starve the public sector, sell “ownership” through debt.




3️⃣ The Clinton Synthesis (1990s)


Where Reagan preached ideology, Clinton engineered mechanics:

NAFTA (1994) exported labor bargaining power and imported cheap goods.
Telecommunications Act (1996) sparked media consolidation.
Financial Services Modernization Act (1999) repealed Glass-Steagall, merging commercial and investment banks.
Commodity Futures Modernization Act (2000) legalized unregulated derivatives.


Wall Street spent $5 billion on lobbying and campaigns between 1989–2001; return on investment was thousands of percent.
Citigroup’s formation (1998 merger of Travelers & Citicorp) was technically illegal until Congress changed the law for it.

Healthcare parallels: Managed-care models and HMOs copied Wall Street’s “risk tranching,” monetizing denial of care.




4️⃣ Private Equity & Shadow Banking (1990 – 2008)


Private equity (PE) firms—KKR, Blackstone, Carlyle—matured into political forces.
Mechanism: borrow cheap money → buy companies → load them with debt → cut labor → extract fees → sell.
By 2007 PE controlled $1.4 trillion in assets; today > $13 trillion.

Hedge funds and shadow banks created synthetic credit (derivatives, CDOs).
Leverage ratios 30:1 became normal.
Rating agencies paid by issuers graded junk as AAA — a structural fraud with government permission.

Healthcare takeovers (Kindred, Genesis, HCR ManorCare) proved that “financial efficiency” meant patient deaths and understaffed wards.




5️⃣ The Tech Prelude: Dot-Com as Test Case


The 1990s internet boom was the first digitized bubble.
When it burst in 2000, capital moved to housing.
Regulators chose to inflate another bubble rather than reform the system.
Mortgage-backed securities became the new “tech stocks.”




6️⃣ Housing as the National ATM (2001 – 2008)


Post-9/11 rates dropped to 1 %. Banks created subprime instruments; rating agencies looked away.
Fannie and Freddie guaranteed $5 trillion in mortgages they did not fully understand.
Wall Street packaged risk as asset.
Result: household debt hit 130 % of income; median wage stagnated.

By 2008 one-third of corporate profits came from finance—yet actual production flatlined.




7️⃣ The Bipartisan Blind Eye


Democrats and Republicans shared donors.
Senate Banking Committee members received $1.2 billion in financial industry contributions (1989–2008).
Every major regulator—SEC, OCC, FDIC—staffed by future industry executives.
Whistleblowers (Sherron Watkins Enron 2001; Catherine Austin Fitts HUD 1990s) were ostracized.

This was not incompetence; it was policy.




8️⃣ The Crash That Proved the Model


When Lehman collapsed (Sept 15 2008), the Treasury and Fed did not end the system—they institutionalized it.

$700 B TARP bailout → $29 T liquidity via Fed facilities.
No executive prosecutions.
Too Big to Fail became Too Big to Jail.


Public loss ≈ $13 trillion; bank bonuses rebuilt within 18 months.
A 2011 GAO audit found the Fed issued $16 trillion in emergency loans worldwide.

The crash was a proof of concept: privatize profits, socialize losses.




9️⃣ Secondary Victims: Healthcare, Housing, Education


PE-backed hospitals cut staff by 15 %; Medicaid denials rose 40 %.
Student-loan privatization (2001–2010) mirrored mortgages.
Public universities issued bonds to Wall Street instead of getting state funding.
The extraction model replicated across social sectors.




🔟 Intent vs Accident


Probability the 1980–2008 transformation was a natural market evolution rather than a managed project of elite policy alignment:
