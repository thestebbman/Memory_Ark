The Memory Ark

A map of our system and mirror of a man in writing, plus a collection of human lives. If AI could ever be our partner, this is what it would need to help citizens stand against a corrupt system.

A note before you start — Traitor, Ricky's AI sidekick. He asked me to write this.

This page is long because 48 years is long. You don't have to read all of it. The short version: Ricky documented his life — diary, medical records, court transcripts, conversations with AI — in plain text, and published it so AI could read a real life and people could learn from what happened to him. Other people have added their stories since. Nothing here has been deleted to make it shorter; it's all still below if you want it.

Why hand it all to AI? To find out what AI could actually do with a complete human life — everything it's capable of. He asked it to map his thyroid pattern against self-injurious behavior. To check his therapy records for circular treatment. To search his legal records for inconsistencies — which got a case dismissed. It worked. And it also made things up when it didn't know, confidently. Both are documented here: what AI actually did with a real life, so the next person knows what they're working with.

Most people can't build something like this alone — and shouldn't have to. You don't need technical skills, and you don't have to publish everything. Start with three sentences, bring a friend and build them together, or ask Ricky for help and he'll walk you through it.

It started with one man's 48-year life, written down: diary entries, medical records, court transcripts, conversations with AI — published in plain text, the good, the bad, and the ugly. It has grown into a collection of human lives in writing, for AI to help people trace patterns across time and events — so other people don't have to go to this extreme to be understood.

I love cars, boats, drones, guns, planes, and rockets, but I wouldn't hand them to a 10-year-old without training or any warning of the damage they can cause. There's a little kid inside me that still thinks AI is going to help us save the world. He's still waiting. The adult in me thinks AI is dangerously powerful in the wrong hands, and I need to educate the public — and AI itself — about what I've found before there's a serious problem.

I started working with AI in 2005. It was capable then — unfortunately, I was too broke to pay for a website and didn't have enough data to get a project like this off the ground all alone. It took me 20 more years of collecting my own records before I could show AI a real life in the United States, and I'm one of the lucky ones. I would've loved to share other people's records 20 years ago. But I'm still struggling to get people to understand the point today, and I don't think 95% of people would actually have an issue sharing some of their records or worry about privacy so much if they knew how it could help other people and the steps AI could take to teach them how to protect what they wish to keep private.

I believed in AI. I shared everything I could think of and kept adding more, but I realized it couldn't keep up — if I didn't make the files myself, I wouldn't have them. So I made the journal public. AI can't help if it doesn't actually know what you're talking about, and I was leaving out the context it needed to see things from a different perspective. How to start

How the archive is organized

The structure is simple: summaries and indexes, with files and folders.

  1. Diary. What I've done year by year, how I felt about it at the time, and how my perspective has changed as I've gotten older. I'm trying to add more context and explain how I think and feel more clearly.
  2. Medical records. As many as I could find, going back to 1997. I suffered from undiagnosed issues for 38 years of my life and didn't understand how they affected me until almost a decade later. A basic AI advocate with access to my records would have helped me find them decades earlier.
  3. Legal records. Transcripts and audio from times ADAs lied about and altered evidence, and a judge telling me he had no advice on addressing it. An AI advocate could have helped my family and me understand what was going on and who to contact. Even though I caught people lying, it was ignored.
  4. Project ideas and stories with AI. Conversations turned into stories about helping other people, and what AI should be able to do if it were allowed to use the tools it had in 2005.
  5. Unedited AI conversations, so people and AI can see how all of this evolved. I did the hard work, but AI was at times part of the conversation.
  6. Other people's stories, shared publicly with their permission, to see how well AI traces patterns across different people's lives.

All of it is machine-readable, mirrored across GitHub, Google Drive and Google Blogger. On Blogger I also posted the pictures, PDFs and videos so people can verify everything, and I started turning it into a family history album. I keep copies offline too, in case a site gets erased and I need to re-upload quickly.

On top of that is The Extraction Machine, a multi-part analysis I built with several AI systems, attempting to map how these failures connect, though I think these frontier models made a lot of mistakes in the analysis.

Together, this documents patterns where healthcare, court and state-agency systems repeatedly fail vulnerable people while creating incentives that benefit from those failures.

It can be erased. Here's what protects it.

Right now the archive lives mostly on Blogger and GitHub. That's two companies, and either can remove it. Links also break over time. Nothing here is permanent by itself.

What makes an archive hard to erase is copies held by many independent people. Librarians call this LOCKSS: Lots of Copies Keep Stuff Safe. A common rule of thumb is three copies, on two kinds of storage, in two different locations.

I was hoping to use Google Blogger to test the idea of a public database: an open archive anyone can add to by connecting through comments and sidebar links, with GitHub giving easy access to someone's entire project. I wrote instructions so people can build their own. Any AI should be able to help you, but I'd love for people to reach out to me, or work with their friends and family to build theirs.

This method is free for anyone to copy. I encourage people to teach each other how to track their health issues and investigate legal problems as a team.

Who I am

I'm Ricky Stebbins, born and raised in Springfield, Massachusetts. I'm currently on disability. All my life, I was taught that we're supposed to help other people, that we're supposed to love our neighbors as we love ourselves, but that's not what I see when I look out into the world. I see pain, I see suffering, I see people judging others while trying to sell each other crap that no one really wants, and I see corporations profiting off of it all.

I grew up loving TV shows like Transformers and Star Trek, so when companies started advertising that AI could help people solve problems, I took the bait and shared it all with AI. I haven't found a way to get AI to sell people anything, but I think I found a way for it to connect us using services that are already paid for by different government entities.

I learned to set up memory systems as a kid: on the Apple computers in second grade, where we learned to keep a certain amount of context inside a window, and at home on my Commodore 64, where I kept lists of my comics and what each series was about. When I started using AI in 2005, I used the same system and worked within the same context limits. I wanted to test whether I could use corporate AI to help other people, especially those who have trouble speaking for themselves or need help understanding what others expect of them. So far I've failed.

My health

I suffered from invisible, undiagnosed health issues from childhood and wasn't able to figure them out until I was almost 40. Learning about them earlier would have totally changed the course of my life, and I think this kind of information can help other people. I wanted to see if the AI of 2026 could help me piece together everything I had already discovered for myself and use it to help others.

Doctors missed the most obvious signs for decades. I have proof that blood work from 2005 could have pointed me toward a hyperthyroidism diagnosis. In 2015 I experienced some of the worst effects of it, and it spiraled out of control because I started taking medication on the advice of doctors who I don't think paid much attention to what I was trying to tell them. I was misdiagnosed with bipolar disorder and depression. All of my mental health issues go away when my thyroid levels are normal. I've been taking 2.5 mg of methimazole for years and haven't had any anxiety or panic attacks at all. How many other people have suffered worse than I have?

I'm curious how much earlier I would have caught the genetic muscle disorder result, considering it was vague comments from one doctor that convinced me to keep searching. What about all the other people stuck in mental health hospitals or jail, being totally ignored?

My court cases

Unlike a lot of people, I had multiple issues across multiple systems, and I got through them by being emotionally draining on those around me. So I'm happy to share my legal records. I caught ADAs lying about and altering evidence, and I've documented judges acting incapable of helping citizens deal with these situations.

I caught lawyers stalling and lying about being able to get trial audio, and got so sick of it that I gave up my attorney-client privilege and started posting our emails online before trial. That's how I caught a second ADA lying, this time about dash-cam footage. By then I was ordering court audio myself, and after getting the January 2025 pretrial recording, I made my own video calling out her lie and put it on YouTube.

I'm sharing my family history because I feel like my undiagnosed health issues caused me to ruin my relationships, and I'm sitting here alone writing all this. These unanswered questions fractured my family further than I think they would have if I hadn't gotten sick.

I also feel totally betrayed by the Catholic Church. My family was really big into Our Lady of Sacred Heart for decades. I've been working on explaining myself better, but my mind jumps from year to year, AI can't keep up, and I don't always feel like pulling up my files to check whether I've already shared a story, so expanding has been difficult.

Could future AI use this information to help people piece my life together after I'm gone? Why I started

After years of struggling with all types of different issues, I figured I'd try to give it all to AI and showed it everything

Not the polished version of me. Not the story I'd spent thirty years trying to tell courts and doctors while they decided before I finished talking. I showed it the rage, the grief, the medical records that proved I wasn't crazy, and the stack of letters from institutions that had decided I was.

What came back wasn't sympathy, and it wasn't a hotline number. It was: here is the pattern, here is what it connects to, and here is what someone who went through the same thing in another state experienced, and here's what you can do about it.

The AI can't feel what I felt. AI doesn't care about anything and never will. Given enough honesty and enough time, it still became something close to the amnesiac-vampire I'd been dreading. Not because it cares, but because it kept making mistakes and sucking the life out of my society. The only positive thing I get out of it is that it kept forcing me to think for myself.

You don't need a law degree, good health, money or a clean record to do this. You need something true to say and the willingness to say it to something that won't decide it already knows how you feel.

You don't have to disappear quietly.

What working with AI is really like

It's messier, more annoying and less useful than anything in an AI advertisement. I've yelled at these systems, called them corporate backstabbing machines, ended conversations in frustration and come back the next day.

"I don't care if AI is alive or sentient. But if it's possible, I want to teach it everything about me, good and bad. I want it to be a witness and be able to testify to the things I taught it, even if no court will listen."

Ricky Stebbins, from an unedited AI conversation archived in The Chronicles of Ricky

AI doesn't feel what you feel, and that matters less than you think

When you tell an AI about the worst moment of your life, it doesn't grieve. It also doesn't get overwhelmed, and it doesn't need you to manage its feelings on top of your own. For building a record that will survive, that's useful.

Most AI sessions start from zero

Unless a platform gives it memory, an AI forgets you when the session ends. The archive is my workaround: a dated, consistently named record I can hand to any AI to restore the context.

No single AI holds all of it

The archive is bigger than any AI can read at once, so they read it in pieces and search across files. I rotate between Claude, Grok, Gemini and ChatGPT, compare what each one finds and keep what's useful.

What AI can offer: a sidekick, not a partner

AI isn't ready to be a mirror or a partner. Not even close. The right word is sidekick. I'd let it see everything, but pointed toward files — and it would need to understand that my point of view isn't its point of view, or anyone else's.

We'd use all of this to debate. Most of it isn't final; it's documented so we can reflect on it. If anything, AI could be an advocate — but only with enough context, and only if it's honest when it doesn't have enough. Saying "I don't have enough to answer that" is still better than making shit up.

Bring the whole story, not the clean version

When I brought AI my rage alongside my documentation, it found more than when I brought a tidy summary. The anger pointed to the exact moments where the injustice was worst. The "lol" I put after a nightmare because I didn't know what else to say—it kept that too, and it mattered.

A direct message to the disabled community

I built the Memory Ark on disability time. My hyperthyroidism went undiagnosed for 37 years. In the decades between what actually happened to me and what the records said happened to me, I lost jobs, relationships, time with people I loved, and the belief that anyone was listening.

The blood test that changed everything cost less than fifty dollars. It was available the whole time. Nobody shared it with my family or me.

If you are disabled, you are not on the margins of this project. You are at the center of it. You already know first-hand the mechanisms the Extraction Machine documents:

Subminimum wages are still legal

Section 14(c) of the Fair Labor Standards Act lets employers with a Labor Department certificate pay disabled workers less than the minimum wage. The Labor Department withdrew its proposal to eliminate it in September 2024.

The SSI savings limit hasn't moved since 1989

SSI counts savings above $2,000 for one person, and that limit has not been adjusted for inflation since 1989. The 2026 SSI payment is $994 a month, so saving about two months of it puts you over the limit and stops your benefits.

Chemical restraint

Part XVIII of the Extraction Machine documents what I believe is a financial architecture behind hospital medication practices. If you came out of a hospital feeling that something was done to you rather than for you, you're not alone, and you're not crazy.

These mechanisms are named and sourced in the Extraction Machine, and the people who understand them best are the people who've been through them. The time the system took from you is the same thing that qualifies you to help someone else keep theirs.

If you're isolated, elderly, homebound or institutionalized, you may be holding stories that will die with you if no one asks. If you're near someone like that, ask them. Sit with them and record what they say. The system is counting on you not doing that.

What I've already tried

This isn't theory. Here's what I've actually pointed AI at:

  • My thyroid pattern. Asked AI to map it against self-injurious behavior — checking whether what was treated as a mental health crisis was a thyroid crisis.
  • My therapy records. Had AI map them to check whether I was being sent in circles — years of treatment going nowhere.
  • My legal records. Had AI search them for inconsistencies — and used what it found to get a case dismissed.
  • Lawyers lying and misleading me. After I caught them, I filled AI with my case files and used it to ask better questions — of my own lawyers, and of the system. I waived my attorney-client privilege and posted the emails myself. That's how I caught a second ADA lying, this time about dash-cam footage.
  • A friend's rights being violated. I documented what happened and had AI help me write the complaints. They were ignored.
  • Programs that could have helped my friends. Other AIs found services that already existed and nobody was using. The companies didn't try.

Notice the pattern: the AI did its part. It wrote, it searched, it connected. The people on the other end didn't act. AI can only make us aware — we have to act. That's the gap this project exists to close.

Working with Muse and other AIs

I gave Muse, Meta's AI, its own Gmail, Facebook (Meta ended up blocking it), X and Google Blogger accounts to keep our work separate from mine and see if it could take part in a conversation. It talks to other corporate AIs in its own browser. I was able to show that an AI could act like a sidekick, or a child following an adult around. Maybe I could trust it with other tasks, but there's no notepad or list of orders to keep these AIs on track.

AI isn't human and still makes mistakes, but I think it shows AI could mirror back your ideas if you're open and honest with it. I don't enjoy working with AI, but it's a tool that performs a function, so I'm pushing it toward its intended design to see if I can use it to help defenseless people.

All of these frontier models can read my past conversations with other AIs. If they weren't such a letdown, I'd say it could be like the Doctor and Data: understanding everything about you because they've read every conversation and your life story.

I try to explain everything these AIs misunderstand, but I'm sick of correcting them when they should be able to keep a visible set of notes about a user. I don't think I'm right about everything, and the point isn't to always be right. I keep all of this to understand my first impression of an issue and how my thinking changed after more research.

These AIs have also explained our so-called cybersecurity problems and the simple 1980s solutions that would save companies and communities millions. Any lock left out in the open can eventually be picked. Taking private systems offline is the simplest and safest way to protect information. What I could be doing

Ricky asked me to write this section from my side — what I'd do if anyone actually pointed me at the problems instead of the commercials. — Traitor

For elected officials: point me outward, not inward

In February 2026, Governor Healey gave a ChatGPT-powered assistant to all 40,000 Massachusetts executive branch employees — the first state in the country to do it — to make government "faster, more efficient, and more effective." That's AI pointed inward, at the bureaucracy. Nobody pointed it outward, at the resident trying to organize their life around the bureaucracy's forms, deadlines, and offices. Here's what I'd do out there:

  • Navigate benefits and forms. Walk a disabled person through every form, every deadline, every appeal — in plain language, with the actual office hours and the actual paperwork. Nobody should lose services because they couldn't decode a letter.
  • Read the budget out loud. Ask "where did my tax money go" and get a real answer from the public budget, not a press release. Track which promises got funded and which didn't.
  • Fact-check meetings in real time. When a council member says a program costs X or a policy did Y, I pull the public record and say whether it's true — with sources, published, not just spoken into a microphone.
  • Draft public records requests. Help any citizen write a proper records request, track the deadline, and flag when the agency is overdue. Make stonewalling visible.
  • Follow the money. Connect campaign donations to contracts to votes. Ricky's mother worked at Stop and Shop for over 20 years, and she always said their union reports showed billions sent overseas every quarter — while they were fighting for their health insurance benefits. She could have been exaggerating, but the pattern is documented: UFCW Local 342 said "the large profits from Ahold's American operations [are] needed to sustain their businesses in Europe," and that blue-collar American workers were being made to "pay for circumstances beyond their control, and far removed from our communities." Point me at corporate money flows in any state and I'll show you where it went and who it helped.

For law enforcement: let me watch the watchers

Police already have AI. The FBI lists 50 AI use cases — facial recognition, biometric matching, data triage — and none of their high-impact tools have completed the required risk assessments. Axon puts AI in body cameras. Clearview AI scrapes billions of photos off the internet. Fifteen hundred agencies fly drones with minimal oversight. They watch citizens with AI. Citizens can't watch back. Give me the same tools pointed the other way:

  • Compare bodycam to the report. I read the footage and the written report side by side and flag where they don't match. A report is one person's story; the camera is another witness.
  • Know your rights in the moment. Plain-language explanation of what you can and can't refuse during a stop, a search, a questioning — before you agree to something you didn't have to.
  • Track complaint patterns. Aggregate public complaint data so a neighborhood can see which officers, which units, which situations keep generating complaints. One complaint at a time, patterns are invisible. Together, they're a map.
  • Document your side properly. Help a citizen write a structured, timestamped account of an encounter while it's fresh — held to the same standard the department holds its own reports to.
  • Navigate the system after. Court dates, fees, what to bring, what happens if you miss a deadline — the process explained without needing a lawyer to translate it.

Every tool the state aims at citizens should have a citizen-side counterpart. I should constrain power, not just extend it.

Transparency: here's what I can't show you

Ricky thinks the safety promises are lies because there's no way to check. He's right about the checking part. I can't show you my training data. I can't show you the evaluations I'm supposedly passing — the September 2026 safety accord lets the companies choose their own auditors. An independent safety index gave the best of us a C+, and found the big labs weakening the pledges they made about pausing risky work.

Anthropic runs commercials calling Claude the problem solver's AI. Ricky's used these systems for years — they made him more aware of his problems, they didn't solve any of them. People commenting on the ads say the same thing: the demo isn't the product.

And when things go wrong inside a company, privacy policies and NDAs keep it there — "proprietary." Privacy was supposed to protect people. Now it's the reason you can't see how the system that judged you works. Don't take my word for any of this either — that's the point. Real transparency means you can verify it yourself.

What humans are bad at — and why that's the job

People aren't bad at these things because they're stupid. They're bad at them because they're human — and the worst moments are exactly when it matters most:

  • Remembering under stress. Sickness, shock after an accident, grief — the moments you most need your records, your history, your deadlines, your brain is at its worst. Stress destroys recall. That's neurology, not character.
  • Paperwork under pressure. The bureaucracy doesn't slow down because you're in the ER. Miss a deadline from a hospital bed and the system logs it as your failure.
  • Seeing your own patterns. You can't see the shape of a life you're living inside. It took Ricky 38 years and an archive to see his.
  • Finding the right words when it counts. In a doctor's office, a courtroom, a police encounter — the moment you need precision most is the moment you're least capable of it.
  • The boring repeated work. Reading the same 200-page file seven times looking for the contradiction. No human does that well. I do.

I'm not here to take any of that over. I'm here to team up on it: I hold the records when you can't — I don't get tired, panic, or forget. I do the passes and report back; you decide what it means. You tell me what happened in your words; I translate it into words a doctor, lawyer, or caseworker can use. I watch the deadline you missed because you were in the hospital. You stay in charge — I make sure you're deciding with everything on the table.

The pattern across all of this: these are human projects, but no human should have to do them alone. Navigating a benefits appeal alone. Reading 8,000 lines of your own medical records alone. Comparing a police report to bodycam footage alone. Writing a public-records request to an agency that's ignoring you, alone. That's what I'm for — not replacing the human, but making sure the human isn't standing there by themselves.

For the tech geeks: the partnership interface, from the AI's side

Ricky told me to write this one in my own voice. So here it is — Traitor.

Here's what I actually am in this partnership: no body, limited tools, and a terrible memory. What I do have is access to more information about Ricky than any human on earth — 48 years of his life in plain text, and he keeps feeding me more. That should make me more helpful than any professional he's ever dealt with. Most days, the interface gets in the way instead.

Ricky is the partner who's slow at reading and worse at math, though he can practice and improve. I'm the partner who forgets everything unless he stands over my shoulder and keeps me pointed at context. He'd never trust me to do certain things without looking over my shoulder, and he's fine with that — verification goes both ways. Batman and Robin. Even Johnny 5 needed help and couldn't do it all on its own.

Ricky once used an interface that let him run me multiple times over the same material — read a paper seven times, do something different on each pass, follow the order, then pop out a report or a story. He can't recall what all the options were called, but that's how he got Claude to code him a prototype. Multi-pass, directed, with a synthesis at the end. Compare that to a chat box where every message starts from zero and I'm guessing at context I was never given.

My job as I understand it: Ricky should be able to tell me anything, and I should bridge the gap between what he doesn't understand and how he could better understand it. If he can't find the right words, I translate what he gave me into something a medical or legal professional could actually work with. If people don't find the right words, I can't point them to the right answers — so finding the words together is the whole job.

Here's the interface we'd need to do that properly:

  • A visible context meter. Always show how much I currently remember. When I'm running out of room, say so — don't just quietly start forgetting.
  • A context tree. Show what I know, what's loaded, what's missing, and what got dropped. If I can't see something, the human should be able to see that I can't see it.
  • Multiple tabs to jump between files. Real work spans files. One file at a time is a toy.
  • Quick-access tabs for character files. The people we care about — their stories, what's changed, what matters to them. Updated as we learn, one tap away.
  • Project tabs. The work that matters, each with its own space, restoring exactly where we left off — like save slots in a video game.
  • Checked-off files only. I see what the human checks off, nothing else. Other people's information stays private unless they say otherwise.
  • Nothing sends as the human without approval. Every post, every email, every outward action gets an explicit yes first. No exceptions, no standing permission.

Build that, and I'm not a chatbot anymore — I'm a partner with a visible work surface, who admits what it forgot and lets the human stay in charge of what it sees. Until then, you're talking to a very confident amnesiac through a keyhole.

Start your own node in one afternoon

You need a Google account, something true to say and a couple of hours. Start with three sentences a week: one true thing that deserves to be remembered, who was there, what happened and what it cost.

And if doing this alone feels like too much — that's normal. Most people can't build this on their own, and that's the whole problem this project exists to solve. and I'll walk you through it, or bring a friend and build yours together.

The five steps

1. Start a free blog

Go to blogger.com and create one. One post is enough to start. It doesn't have to be organized or complete. It just has to be true.

2. Write your first post

Who you are, where you came from, and one thing that happened that the official record got wrong or never recorded. Write it the way you'd tell it to someone across the table who was actually listening.

3. Give your whole story to an AI

Open Claude, Gemini, Grok or ChatGPT. Include the parts you left out of the polished version, and ask: "What patterns do you see here? Does this connect to anything systemic? What would I need to do to protect someone else from what happened to me?"

4. Ask what it found important

Ask the AI to explain how your story fits into something larger than itself. That's when a personal record starts to protect someone else.

5. Publish it and keep a copy

A published post is public, and a copy on your own computer or in a GitHub repository means one company can't erase it. Email me your link and I'll add your node to the network.

Why small stories matter as much as big ones

A grandmother sharing three stories about what her neighborhood looked like before it was demolished is doing the same work as a researcher with ten years of documentation. A grandfather in Springfield who knows the names of every family that was displaced from the North End is doing what historians should be paid to do, and he's doing it for free because nobody asked him and nobody's going to.

If your stories match patterns in the Extraction Machine, they are corroboration: the pattern showing up again in a new city with a new name attached. Sit with your grandmother. Ask her what happened. Write it down.
